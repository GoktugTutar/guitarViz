Chord progressions

A progression connects chords over time. Roman numerals describe the pattern, so you can move it to another key without changing its basic structure.

Try it on the fretboard →

What do the Roman numerals mean?

A Roman numeral identifies the scale degree a chord is built on. Uppercase means major, lowercase means minor, and ° means diminished. In a major key, the seven diatonic triads are I–ii–iii–IV–V–vi–vii°.

To build a triad, start on a scale note and take every other note until you have three. In G major, starting on G gives G–B–D (G major). Starting on A gives A–C–E (A minor).

Example: I–V–vi–IV in G major

A four-chord progression in G
DegreeChordNotesRole
IGG–B–DTonic: home
VDD–F♯–ADominant: tension
viEmE–G–BRelative minor
IVCC–E–GSubdominant: departure

Play each chord for four beats, then repeat. In C major the same numerals give C–G–Am–F. The chord names change while the scale-degree pattern stays the same.

Minor keys: why v and V sound different

In this app, minor-key Roman numerals are numbered relative to the natural minor scale: i–ii°–III–iv–v–VI–VII. In A minor, i–iv–v gives Am–Dm–Em.

Changing v to major V gives Am–Dm–E. The E major chord contains G♯ instead of G. This raised seventh of A minor is a leading tone, a semitone below A, and gives the return to Am a stronger pull. It comes from A harmonic minor.

Listen and follow the changes

  1. Open Progressions, choose G and Major, and select I–V–vi–IV.
  2. Tap a chord card to see its notes, role and a playable position.
  3. Set the tempo and press Play progression. Each chord lasts four beats.
  4. Enable Loop to repeat, or use the position selector to explore other fingerings while stopped.
  5. Open the explanation below the cards to inspect all seven chords in the key.

The ii–V–I preset uses triads. You can separately explore seventh-chord colors in Chord Finder, including minor 7, dominant 7 and major 7.
